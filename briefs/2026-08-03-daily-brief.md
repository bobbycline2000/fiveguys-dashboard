### 2. Prompt of the Week

Use this next time a staff member drops a problem in your lap that needs documenting and a plan:

```
You are a restaurant operations problem-solver. I'm going to describe a situation that happened at my location. Your job is to:

1. Summarize what went wrong in one sentence.
2. Break down the ROOT CAUSE (not just the symptom).
3. Give me 3 specific, actionable steps to prevent it next time.
4. Draft a short message I can use when talking to the team about this.

Be direct. No fluff. I'll paste in the situation now.
```

Then paste in whatever happened — missed deposit, food waste spike, a customer service miss, a scheduling conflict that tanked coverage.

Why this works: The prompt forces Claude to separate signal from noise. It trains you to think in root causes (not just reactions), gives you talking points for your team in the team's own voice, and gets you from "this sucks" to "here's what we do different" in under 2 minutes. You run this three times a week and watch your staff's recurring problems shrink.

### 3. Use Case Spotlight

**The problem:** You get a weekly food cost email from corporate. It's a PDF. You know something's off (beef usage looks high, waste report is up) but the email just gives you totals — no breakdown of *why*.

**The old move:** Forward it to your COGS email, do some Excel sleuthing, or just live with the confusion until the next variance meeting.

**The Claude move:** Screenshot the PDF. Paste into Claude with this line: "My food cost is 34.2% this week (budget 31%). What's the most likely culprit — portion size creep, waste, theft, or vendor price spike? What's the best first move to diagnose?" Claude will walk you through the math and give you the three things to check *first*.

Takes 60 seconds. You catch problems before they compound. That's the difference between "we'll address it next week" and "I fixed it Wednesday."

### 4. Gotcha of the Week

**The Trap:** You ask Claude "What's a good labor percentage for a fast casual restaurant?" Claude gives you an answer. It sounds confident. It probably sounds right. Except it might be dead wrong for *your store*, *your market*, *your volume*, *your staffing model*.

Claude is giving you an *average* dressed up as advice. Averages kill operators who treat them like targets.

**The Fix:** Replace the vague question with facts. "My store did $18K in sales last week. I had $3,200 in total labor (crew + me). I'm in Louisville, staffing model is skeleton crew on slow days, full team on weekend. What should I actually target?" Now Claude can give you something *specific* instead of generic.

Same thing applies to food cost, check average, cover count, any benchmark. Vague question → vague answer. Specific context → useful answer. Every time.
